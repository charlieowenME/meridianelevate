Is B2B Cold Calling Outdated Or Are We Just Doing It Wrong?

Is B2B cold calling dead? After 12 years working in outbound sales, I don't believe it is. What I do believe is outdated is the idea that the purpose of every cold call is to reach a decision-maker, deliver a pitch and book a meeting.

Ten years ago, reaching the right person might have taken a handful of calls. Today, businesses have better gatekeepers, more complex structures and far more ways of protecting senior decision-makers from unsolicited sales approaches.

If your entire cold-calling strategy depends on getting through to the person at the top, every unanswered call feels like a failure. I look at it differently. Cold calling should be treated as reconnaissance. Every conversation is an opportunity to understand the business, its operation, its people and whether there is actually a commercial opportunity worth pursuing.

Stop Treating Every Call as a Pitch

One of the biggest problems with traditional cold calling is how success is measured. A salesperson makes 80 calls, reaches ten decision-makers and books one meeting. The remaining calls are often treated as unsuccessful activity.

But what if those calls were used to understand the accounts instead? Rather than repeatedly trying to get through to the Supply Chain Director, those conversations can help establish how the operation works, who is involved, what provider is currently being used, where the problems are and who inside the business experiences those problems every day.

Find the People Who Live With the Problem

The decision-maker isn't always the person with the most valuable information.

Take logistics as an example. A Supply Chain Director may ultimately have authority over a 3PL relationship, but they aren't necessarily dealing with the operational problems every day.

The Warehouse Manager might be the person dealing with an off-the-shelf warehouse management system that doesn't integrate properly with the business. They're dealing with transport failures, process problems, workarounds and the frustration those issues create for the people actually running the operation.

They might have no authority whatsoever to replace the provider. That doesn't make them irrelevant. In many cases, it makes them one of the most valuable people you can speak to.

The "Golden Goose"

One of the profiles I look for is what I call a "Golden Goose".

Imagine an experienced Warehouse Manager who has recently moved from a large, highly structured company into a smaller growing operation. They've seen what good infrastructure looks like. They know how a larger operation handles systems, fulfilment, transport and integration.

Six months into the new role, they're also beginning to understand exactly where the smaller operation struggles.

I'm not calling that person and immediately asking them to help me replace their 3PL. That's a sales pitch, and it's far too early. I'd rather ask about their move. How have they found the difference between the two operations? What's been better? What's been harder?

Experienced operational people will talk about what they've seen. The objective isn't to interrogate them. It's to have a genuine conversation, listen and gradually understand the operation from somebody standing close to the pallets, warehouse doors, systems and daily problems.

Don't Ask Every Question on the First Call

This is where traditional qualification can become counterproductive. If you've just started speaking to somebody and immediately ask when their supplier contract renews, the conversation changes.

They know they're being qualified for a sale.

I would rather build the information gradually across several conversations. Start by understanding the operation. Understand how established the current provider relationship is. Listen for the operational differences and frustrations. Only once familiarity develops should the conversation naturally move towards who owns the decision and when change could realistically happen.

The important point is that qualification should be the result of the conversation , not the reason the conversation feels like it exists.

"Operational staff tell you where the pain is. Your job as a salesperson is to understand whether that pain creates a commercial reason to change."

Operational Pain Isn't Automatically a Business Case

This distinction matters.

A Warehouse Manager might hate a particular system or provider. That doesn't automatically mean changing it makes commercial sense. Sometimes an operational headache is simply the compromise a business has made to use a cheaper solution.

Before approaching the senior decision-maker, the intelligence needs to be put together with proper qualification and competitor analysis. The question becomes bigger than whether the warehouse team is frustrated.

Does the current structure affect efficiency? Is it restricting growth? Could it be contributing to lost revenue or unnecessary costs elsewhere? And if the business grows significantly, what happens to problems that already exist today?

Operational Complaint

"The current system is frustrating."

A genuine problem for the people using it, but not necessarily enough on its own to justify changing provider.

Commercial Case

"The current structure could restrict profitable growth."

Now the conversation is about the bottom line, scalability and the wider structure of the business.

By the Time I Speak to the Decision-Maker, It Shouldn't Feel Cold

Once the account has been properly pre-qualified, the likelihood of getting a meaningful conversation with the decision-maker changes dramatically.

I'm no longer introducing a generic service and hoping something sticks. I understand the operation, the current structure, the problems and the potential commercial impact. I can then analyse competitors and build a proposition around what that specific business may need.

Importantly, I wouldn't walk into that conversation and repeat everything the Warehouse Manager has told me. That risks damaging the relationship with the person who gave me the operational insight.

Instead, the senior conversation should be built around the wider commercial picture: how comparable businesses operate, where the current structure may be limiting performance and what a stronger solution could do for revenue, efficiency and future growth.

Meeting Booked Doesn't Mean Opportunity Created

This is where I think a lot of sales organisations get cold calling wrong.

From junior salespeople to senior teams, people are often judged on one number: how many meetings did you book?

The result is predictable. Salespeople are under pressure to force meetings as quickly as possible. Those meetings turn into presentations that go nowhere because the account wasn't properly understood or qualified in the first place.

Personally, I'd rather see 50 useful pieces of pre-qualification intelligence gathered across target accounts and the beginnings of relationships with key people than celebrate one meeting booked for the sake of hitting a KPI.

A Supply Chain Director can agree to a meeting today and barely remember why they accepted it by the time the date arrives. Intelligence and relationships accumulate. That's what creates something a sales team can actually build on.

A call attempt is an activity. A meeting is an event.

Neither automatically represents commercial progress. The question is what your sales team knows about the account today that it didn't know yesterday.

We Also Need to Stop Expecting Every Salesperson to Be the Same

Someone can be exceptional at gathering information, building relationships with operational contacts and understanding an account, but struggle to secure a senior meeting.

That doesn't necessarily make them a bad salesperson.

If that's what they're exceptional at, use it. Let them build the account intelligence. Then bring in the personable senior salesperson who is excellent at engaging the decision-maker and presenting the commercial case.

Sales teams lose good people when everybody is measured against the same narrow definition of success. Junior staff can spend months making calls, identifying contacts and building CRM intelligence, only to burn out and leave while the warmer opportunities and data remain behind for somebody more senior to inherit.

Stop judging every salesperson by whether they can do every part of the sale. Find out which part they're exceptional at and build the process around it.

LinkedIn and Email Haven't Replaced the Phone

LinkedIn and email absolutely have a place in outbound sales, but relying on them alone can become a needle-in-a-haystack strategy.

Send enough emails and eventually you'll find businesses that are actively looking for what you sell. The problem is that when a company is already actively looking, there's a good chance you're walking into an existing buying process alongside several other suppliers.

At that point, price and timing can dominate the conversation.

I would rather identify the passive company that has genuine issues but hasn't yet decided to go to market. Build the relationship, understand the operation and develop the opportunity before a formal buying process begins.

When that business eventually decides to compare suppliers, the competitors may be working from a tender document or completed data form. You could already have months of context, relationships and understanding of the internal pain points behind that document.

"The best time to find an opportunity isn't necessarily when a company starts looking for a supplier. By then, you're already competing for it."

So, Is B2B Cold Calling Outdated?

No. But parts of the way businesses use it are.

If cold calling means hammering through a database, trying to bypass everybody who isn't a director and judging success purely by how many meetings appear in the calendar, then it's understandable why people think the model is broken.

Used strategically, the phone gives you something automated outreach struggles to provide: conversations with the people inside a business who understand how it actually operates.

Those conversations create intelligence. Intelligence creates better qualification. Better qualification allows deeper analysis. And that gives you the ability to approach a decision-maker with something relevant to their business rather than another generic sales pitch.

The goal isn't simply to make more calls. It's to make sure every useful conversation leaves you understanding the account better than you did before you picked up the phone.
